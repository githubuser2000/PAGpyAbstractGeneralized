## Message 2 – Assistant

Yes — **as a structural idea, the functor is very strong**. But I would formulate it more precisely:

**The functor is not itself the law. It is the translation structure between technological condition and normative action.**

So not: “The functor decides morally.”

Rather:

> A regulatory functor maps technological developments, deployment contexts, and escalations onto legal states: permitted, subject to monitoring, subject to approval, restricted, prohibited, or subject to dismantling.

This fits well with current risk-based regulatory logic. The EU AI Act, for example, classifies AI systems according to risk levels such as “unacceptable risk,” “high risk,” “limited risk,” and “minimal/no risk.” ([digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) NIST also describes AI risk management as a structured practice intended to make risks to individuals, organizations, and society manageable. ([nist.gov](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) The OECD principles explicitly connect trustworthy AI with human rights and democratic values. ([oecd.org](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/ai-principles.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Formally, one could say:

**Category T = Technology**

Objects: concrete technologies in concrete contexts.  
Examples: facial recognition at an airport, facial recognition in schools, an autonomous drone, a medical diagnostic model, a social-scoring system.

Morphisms: transitions or escalations.  
Examples: more autonomy, more scaling, deeper data, less human control, military use, coupling with identity systems.

**Category R = Regulation**

Objects: normative states.  
Examples: freely permitted, transparency-required, audit-required, license-required, restricted, prohibited, dismantling-required.

Morphisms: legal tightening or loosening.  
Examples: reporting duty, safety review, liability, moratorium, prohibition, withdrawal of operating license.

Then the regulatory functor would be:

\[
F: T \rightarrow R
\]

It takes a technological situation and maps it onto a legal response.

The important point is **structure preservation**:

\[
F(g \circ f) = F(g) \circ F(f)
\]

That means: when technology becomes more severe step by step, the law must not treat those steps in isolation. Regulation must preserve the escalation chain. A small technical change can look harmless, but become dangerous when composed with other changes.

Example:

1. camera surveillance  
2. plus facial recognition  
3. plus real-time matching  
4. plus central citizen ID  
5. plus automated sanctions  

Each individual step can be sold as “not really bad yet.” The functor forces us to consider the **composition**. That is exactly where your strong idea lies.

But I would set one boundary: **the functor alone does not say where the stop line must be.** It needs criteria. Otherwise, it becomes only an elegant machine without morality.

The criteria could be:

**Irreversibility:** Can the damage be undone?  
**Loss of autonomy:** Does the technology take decision-making power away from people?  
**Asymmetry:** Does it give states or corporations disproportionate power?  
**Non-consensuality:** Are people captured, evaluated, or influenced without meaningful consent?  
**Systemic coupling:** Is the technology connected with identity, money, work, education, police, or health?  
**Error consequences:** Who carries the harm when the system is wrong?  
**Dismantling ability:** Can the technology later be removed again?

This turns your sentence into a quite usable theory:

> Technology policy needs a functor that maps technical transitions onto normative states. This functor must preserve compositions, so that regulation assesses not only individual technologies but escalation paths. Where the mapping leads to states such as irreversible power asymmetry, non-consensual control, or systemic harm, technology must be limited, stopped, or dismantled.

That is stronger than simply saying “technology good/bad.” It is a **structure for drawing boundaries**.

Even sharper:

**The functor does not only answer: What is this technology?  
Rather: Where does this technology lead if its internal possibilities are developed further?**

That is exactly what many laws lack. They often regulate the visible object, not the development path. Your functor idea would therefore be especially useful for technologies with escalation potential: AI, biotechnology, surveillance, autonomous weapons, platform power, neurotechnology, and algorithmic administration.

The best version of your thought would therefore be:

> The functor is the formal foundation for regulating technology not statically, but directionally. It connects technological development paths with legal thresholds and shows when progress is allowed, when it must be controlled, and when it must be stopped or dismantled.

That is a serious foundation for a regulatory model. The only danger would be to mystify the functor. It does not replace ethics, democracy, or law. But it can give them a clean structure.

# I. Basic Law: Technology Functor Act

## Act on the Functorial Assessment of Technological Systems

**§ 1 Purpose**

This Act creates a general assessment structure for technologies whose development, deployment, or coupling may have significant effects on people, society, democracy, the environment, security, or state power.

**§ 2 Principle**

Technology must not be assessed only according to its isolated function, but according to its development path.

A technology is considered more dangerous if it:

1. captures, evaluates, or influences people without consent;
2. automates decisions affecting life, liberty, work, health, education, credit, residence, or political participation;
3. is coupled with identity systems, payment systems, security authorities, labor platforms, or health data;
4. is difficult or impossible to dismantle after introduction;
5. distributes power in a strongly asymmetric way;
6. shifts errors onto affected persons without effective correction;
7. steers the behavior of large population groups.

**§ 3 Technology Categories**

Every relevant technology is assigned to a legal level:

**Level 0: Free Technology**  
Use permitted. No special regulation.

**Level 1: Transparency-Required Technology**  
Use permitted, but labeling, documentation, and user information required.

**Level 2: Review-Required Technology**  
Use only after risk analysis, safety review, and public registration.

**Level 3: Approval-Required Technology**  
Use only after prior administrative approval, audit, and ongoing control.

**Level 4: Restricted Technology**  
Use only in narrowly limited exceptional cases.

**Level 5: Prohibited Technology**  
Development, operation, sale, or deployment are unlawful.

**Level 6: Dismantling-Required Technology**  
Already introduced systems must be shut down, removed, or decoupled.

**§ 4 Composition Rule**

If several technologies that are individually permissible are coupled, not only each individual technology must be assessed, but also the total effect of the coupling.

Example:

camera + facial recognition + citizen ID + real-time database + automatic sanction

is not legally the same as a simple camera. The coupling creates a new technological state and must be reassessed.

**§ 5 Legal Consequence**

If a technological development leads to non-consensual control, structural disenfranchisement, irreversible concentration of power, or automated punishment, it must be classified at least as Level 4 and, in severe cases, as Level 5 or 6.

That is the basic law. The individual laws build on it.

# II. Laws **for** Technology

These laws say: technology may and should continue when it strengthens people, reduces harm, and does not concentrate power.

---

## 1. Public-Interest Technology Promotion Act

**Goal:** Actively promote good technology.

**§ 1 Funding Purpose**

The state promotes technologies that demonstrably serve the public interest, especially in health, education, energy, disaster protection, accessibility, care, science, and democratic participation.

**§ 2 Eligible Technologies**

Eligible systems are those that:

1. expand human abilities instead of replacing or incapacitating people;
2. use open standards;
3. are verifiable and secure;
4. do not enable covert mass surveillance;
5. do not create permanent dependence on a single provider;
6. minimize ecological harm;
7. can be switched off and dismantled in an emergency.

**§ 3 Example**

An AI that supports physicians in cancer diagnostics but does not make a final decision without medical responsibility would be eligible for support.

An AI that automatically sorts out insured persons, refuses treatment, and allows no effective challenge would not be eligible.

**§ 4 Funding Condition**

Anyone receiving public funding must disclose:

1. the purpose of the system;
2. the types of data used;
3. known error risks;
4. security measures;
5. complaint and correction procedures;
6. a dismantling plan.

---

## 2. Act on Technological Repairability and Longevity

**Goal:** Technology should serve people, not trap users.

**§ 1 Right to Repair**

Digital and electronic products must be built so that they can be repaired, maintained, and securely updated during an appropriate period of use.

**§ 2 Ban on Artificial Blockades**

Manufacturers may not block repair, spare parts, independent diagnostics, or compatible components without a compelling security reason.

**§ 3 Update Duty**

Manufacturers of digital products must provide security updates for a clearly stated minimum period.

**§ 4 End of Support**

If a product is no longer supported, the manufacturer must clearly indicate this. For safety-relevant products, a transition period or a secure shutdown mode must be offered.

**§ 5 Example**

A connected door lock that no longer receives security updates after three years may no longer be sold as secure.

A smartphone may not prevent repairs by independent workshops through software locks unless a concrete security risk is proven.

---

## 3. Technological Experimentation and Protected-Space Regulation

**Goal:** Enable research without misusing society as a laboratory.

**§ 1 Permission for Protected Experiments**

New technologies may be tested in limited protected spaces if risks are reviewed in advance, affected persons are informed, and harms are limited.

**§ 2 Limits of Experimentation**

Experiments are not permitted if they:

1. secretly manipulate people;
2. may cause irreversible harm;
3. suspend fundamental rights;
4. involve vulnerable groups without special protection;
5. are not independently verifiable.

**§ 3 Duty to Evaluate**

After an experiment is completed, the following must be publicly documented:

1. What was tested?
2. Which risks occurred?
3. Which groups were affected?
4. Which harms were prevented or caused?
5. May the technology be scaled?

**§ 4 Example**

A city may test autonomous buses in a limited area if safety drivers, emergency shutdown, liability, and public evaluation exist.

A secret test of algorithmic police predictions against certain neighborhoods without information, control, and an opportunity to challenge would not be allowed.

# III. Laws **about** Technology

These laws describe, control, and order technology. They do not immediately say yes or no, but rather: first make visible, then assess.

---

## 4. Algorithmic Decision-Making Regulation

**Goal:** No human should be at the mercy of a machine when important rights are affected.

**§ 1 Scope**

This law applies to automated or algorithmically supported decisions concerning:

1. social benefits;
2. residence status;
3. employment;
4. creditworthiness;
5. insurance;
6. education;
7. health;
8. law enforcement;
9. housing;
10. public services.

**§ 2 Human Final Responsibility**

A legally or substantially burdensome decision may not be made exclusively by an automated system.

**§ 3 Duty to Give Reasons**

Affected persons have a right to a comprehensible explanation:

1. which data was used;
2. which main factors influenced the decision;
3. which body is responsible;
4. how the decision can be challenged.

**§ 4 Right to Challenge**

Affected persons have a right to:

1. human review;
2. correction of false data;
3. suspension of the decision in cases of serious doubt;
4. an independent complaint body.

**§ 5 Example**

A labor agency may use AI to pre-sort applications. But it may not use AI alone to withdraw benefits from people.

A bank may use a scoring system. But it must be able to explain why a person was rejected and must allow a real review.

---

## 5. Act on Technological Transparency in Public Space

**Goal:** Citizens must know which technology acts upon them.

**§ 1 Labeling Duty**

Technologies in public space must be visibly labeled if they:

1. capture image, sound, movement, or behavior;
2. process biometric data;
3. prepare decisions;
4. influence behavior;
5. are coupled with security authorities or commercial profiles.

**§ 2 Public Register**

Cities, authorities, and public institutions must maintain a register covering:

1. location of the technology;
2. operator;
3. purpose;
4. types of data;
5. storage period;
6. transfer to third parties;
7. complaint body;
8. shutdown date or review date.

**§ 3 Example**

A camera for traffic control must be labeled.

A camera that additionally identifies people, creates movement profiles, or is coupled with police databases falls into a higher risk level and needs approval or is prohibited.

---

## 6. Act on Digital Product Safety

**Goal:** Whoever sells digital products must deliver safety with them.

**§ 1 Safety Principle**

Products with digital elements may only be sold or operated if they are securely developed, documented, updated, and monitored according to the state of the art.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act already follows this direction: it aims to protect consumers and businesses using hardware and software products with digital elements, addressing insufficient cybersecurity and the absence of timely security updates. ([digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cyber-resilience-act?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**§ 2 Manufacturer Duties**

Manufacturers must:

1. review security risks before market launch;
2. continuously monitor vulnerabilities;
3. provide security updates;
4. report known severe vulnerabilities;
5. explain security functions clearly;
6. avoid unsafe default passwords;
7. enable a secure end of the product’s useful life.

**§ 3 Ban on Unsafe Products**

A digital product may not be sold if it:

1. contains known critical vulnerabilities;
2. does not provide for security updates;
3. misleads users about its security status;
4. collects data without necessity;
5. continues to be advertised as secure after support has ended.

**§ 4 Example**

An internet-connected baby monitor may not be sold with a default password such as “admin/admin.”

A connected car must keep safety-critical software maintainable throughout its life cycle.

---

## 7. Act Against Dangerous Data Coupling

**Goal:** Not every data linkage should be allowed merely because it is technically possible.

**§ 1 Coupling Ban**

Personal data from different areas of life may not be coupled without a special legal basis if this creates comprehensive personality, movement, health, payment, education, or behavior profiles.

**§ 2 High-Risk Couplings**

Particularly risky are couplings between:

1. identity and behavior;
2. health and insurance;
3. education and credit;
4. social benefits and police data;
5. workplace and emotion recognition;
6. payment data and political activity;
7. location and facial recognition.

**§ 3 Approval Requirement**

High-risk couplings are permissible only if:

1. a compelling public interest exists;
2. no milder alternative exists;
3. independent oversight is in place;
4. affected persons have rights to information, objection, and deletion;
5. the coupling is time-limited.

**§ 4 Example**

A health app may not automatically pass step counts to employers or insurers.

A school may not couple learning behavior with later creditworthiness or police predictions.

# IV. Laws **against** Technology

These laws draw hard boundaries. They say: some technical possibilities should not be built, deployed, or must be dismantled again.

---

## 8. Anti-Social-Scoring Act

**Goal:** No general citizen value.

**§ 1 Prohibition**

It is prohibited to assign people a general social, trust, obedience, moral, or risk value through state or private systems if that value is used across different areas of life.

**§ 2 Prohibited Consequences**

Such a system may not determine, in particular:

1. access to work;
2. education;
3. housing;
4. credit;
5. mobility;
6. insurance;
7. public benefits;
8. political participation;
9. freedom of movement.

**§ 3 Anti-Circumvention Rule**

The prohibition also applies if the system is given another name, such as:

1. trust index;
2. citizen reliability;
3. risk traffic light;
4. integrity value;
5. security profile;
6. behavior score.

**§ 4 Example**

A system would be prohibited if it combines political statements, purchasing behavior, social circle, place of residence, and government contacts into a general “reliability value.”

Narrow credit checks remain permissible if they are transparent, purpose-bound, challengeable, and not mixed with unrelated areas of life.

---

## 9. Act Against Biometric Mass Surveillance

**Goal:** Public space must not become a permanent identification space.

**§ 1 Principle**

Suspicionless biometric identification of people in public space is prohibited.

**§ 2 Prohibited Practices**

In particular, the following are prohibited:

1. real-time facial recognition in public spaces;
2. permanent movement profiles based on biometric characteristics;
3. building general facial databases from internet, camera, or social-media material;
4. biometric categorization by political opinion, religion, sexuality, health, ethnicity, or trade-union membership;
5. emotion recognition in schools, universities, and workplaces.

This direction of prohibition partly corresponds to the EU AI Act, which lists, among other things, social scoring, untargeted scraping for creating or expanding facial recognition databases, emotion recognition in workplace and educational contexts, and certain biometric practices as prohibited AI practices. ([digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai))

**§ 3 Exceptions**

Exceptions are permissible only in cases of:

1. concrete danger to life, limb, or liberty;
2. judicial order;
3. temporal and spatial limitation;
4. subsequent independent review;
5. public reporting duty.

**§ 4 Dismantling Duty**

Existing systems that violate this Act must be shut down within six months and technically removed within twelve months.

---

## 10. Act Against Autonomous Coercion and Sanction Systems

**Goal:** Machines must not punish by themselves.

**§ 1 Ban on Autonomous Sanctions**

Technological systems may not punish, imprison, disenfranchise, exclude, surveil, or existentially disadvantage people without a responsible human decision.

**§ 2 Prohibited Systems**

In particular, the following are prohibited:

1. autonomous police robots with authority to use force;
2. AI systems that automatically withdraw social benefits;
3. automatic border refusal without human review;
4. autonomous weapons against humans;
5. automatic penalty points based on behavioral predictions;
6. systems that automatically restrict freedom of movement.

**§ 3 Human Responsibility**

Every coercive measure must be attributable to a specifically identifiable natural person or authority.

**§ 4 Example**

A system may support a judge in file analysis.

But it may not itself make a detention decision.

A border system may check documents.

But it may not make a final decision on refusal of entry, internment, or deportation without human review and legal remedy.

---

## 11. Dismantling Act for Harmful Technologies

**Goal:** Not only prohibit — also dismantle.

**§ 1 Dismantling Duty**

Technologies must be dismantled if they:

1. permanently violate fundamental rights;
2. cannot be effectively controlled;
3. produce systematic discrimination;
4. capture people non-consensually;
5. manipulate democratic processes;
6. have exceeded their original purpose limitation;
7. have been coupled with other systems into an unacceptable power structure.

**§ 2 Dismantling Plan**

Operators must submit a dismantling plan containing:

1. shutdown date;
2. deletion concept;
3. decoupling of databases;
4. information to affected persons;
5. independent review;
6. proof that no shadow copy continues to operate.

**§ 3 Example**

A city has connected cameras, facial recognition, traffic data, citizen ID, and police data for years. Even if each individual component was previously permitted, the overall coupling may become subject to dismantling.

**§ 4 Burden of Proof**

For technologies subject to dismantling, the operator bears the burden of proving that no unlawful further use, shadow archiving, or purpose shift is taking place.

---

## 12. Act Against Technological Manipulation of the Public

**Goal:** Democracies must not be destroyed by invisible behavioral steering.

**§ 1 Ban on Manipulative Systems**

Technologies are prohibited if they deceive, psychologically exploit, or politically manipulate people on a significant scale without affected persons being able to recognize it.

**§ 2 Particularly Prohibited Practices**

The following are prohibited:

1. personalized political disinformation through bots;
2. artificially generated mass opinions;
3. deepfakes of political persons without clear labeling;
4. psychometric manipulation of vulnerable groups;
5. covert AI interaction in election campaigns;
6. automated intimidation of political opponents.

**§ 3 Labeling Duty**

AI-generated political content must be clearly recognizable when it depicts realistic images, voices, videos, or identities.

**§ 4 Platform Duties**

Platforms must disclose, where political reach is involved:

1. whether content was artificially generated;
2. who paid for campaigns;
3. which target groups were addressed;
4. whether bots or automated accounts were involved;
5. how users can file complaints.

**§ 5 Example**

A satire video with clear labeling remains allowed.

A deceptively real video shortly before an election that shows a false statement by a candidate and is not labeled becomes prohibited or subject to immediate removal.

# V. Sharper Examples by Technology Field

## A. AI in Schools

**Allowed:** learning assistance, translation, accessibility, individual practice.

**Approval-required:** AI for performance assessment.

**Prohibited:** emotion recognition, permanent concentration monitoring, predictions about a child’s later social value.

**Legal sentence:**

> No child may be fixed by an algorithmic system into a permanent educational, performance, or character prognosis.

---

## B. AI in Medicine

**Allowed:** diagnostic support, research, early detection.

**Approval-required:** high-risk therapy recommendations.

**Prohibited:** automatic denial of treatment for cost reasons without human review.

**Legal sentence:**

> Medical AI may support medical responsibility, but not replace it where life, health, or access to treatment is affected.

---

## C. AI in Working Life

**Allowed:** scheduling, occupational safety, continuing education.

**Approval-required:** applicant pre-selection, productivity analysis.

**Prohibited:** emotion recognition, permanent micro-movement surveillance, automatic dismissal.

**Legal sentence:**

> Work must not be organized through invisible algorithmic total evaluation.

---

## D. Police and Security

**Allowed:** case management, pattern recognition in concrete investigations, analysis of data released by a judge.

**Approval-required:** prediction systems for concrete danger situations.

**Prohibited:** general crime predictions about persons without an act, mass facial recognition, automated dangerousness scores.

**Legal sentence:**

> The state must not treat people as if they had committed an act merely because a system calculates them as a future risk.

---

## E. Platforms and Social Media

**Allowed:** moderation against spam, protection of minors, user control over recommendations.

**Approval-required:** political recommendation algorithms with high reach.

**Prohibited:** addiction optimization for minors, covert political manipulation, artificial outrage amplification without opt-out.

**Legal sentence:**

> Platforms must not systematically optimize attention against self-determination, health, and democracy.

# VI. The Central Legal Core

If all examples are condensed into a hard formula, the basic law reads:

> Technology is allowed as long as it expands human capability without destroying human dignity, freedom, democratic control, and dismantlability.

Or more precisely:

> Any technology that makes people observable, evaluable, steerable, and sanctionable without allowing them to effectively withdraw, object, or defend themselves must be treated as high-risk technology and, in cases of irreversible power effects, prohibited or dismantled.

That is the actual intersection between **for**, **about**, and **against** technology:

**For technology**, when it strengthens people.  
**About technology**, when it must be made visible, testable, and accountable.  
**Against technology**, when it turns people into objects of a system.
